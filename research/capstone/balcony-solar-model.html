Balcony solar on PG&E's residential system

A plug-in panel hung off a railing generates a modest amount of power, but spread across millions of households it starts to move real volume. Set the assumptions on the left to see what it does to load and to utility revenue across PG&E's four main residential rate schedules.

Effect by rate schedule

CARE customers pay a lower blended rate, so each displaced kilowatt-hour is worth less to them and costs PG&E less in forgone revenue. The same panel pays back roughly twice as fast on a standard schedule as on a CARE one.

What this model does not capture

  • The fixed charge survives. Starting March 2026 most households pay a Base Services Charge of $24.15 a month, or $6 on CARE and $12 on FERA. Balcony solar reduces volumetric charges only, so roughly $290 a year is untouchable for a non-CARE home no matter how much the panel produces.
  • Full on-site use is the optimistic case. This model credits every kilowatt-hour at the retail rate, which assumes household demand always meets or exceeds what the panel is producing. In practice a midday surplus in an empty home flows to the grid and earns an export credit well below retail, so real savings land somewhat under these figures.
  • Blended rates hide the time dimension. Each schedule here uses a single average rate derived from total revenue divided by total sales. On the time-of-use schedules, solar output peaks near midday while the expensive 4 to 9 p.m. window sits after the sun drops, so real savings on E-TOU-C and EL-TOU-C would run below what is shown.
  • Revenue does not simply vanish. Under California's ratemaking, PG&E's authorized revenue requirement is set in the General Rate Case regardless of sales volume. Balancing accounts true up the difference, which means displaced revenue is recovered later from the remaining customer base rather than lost, an effect at the center of the cost-shift debate.
  • Plug-in solar is not yet straightforward in California. Unlike Germany, where balcony systems are explicitly permitted, the regulatory path here is still unsettled. Treat adoption figures above a few percent as a policy scenario, not a forecast.
