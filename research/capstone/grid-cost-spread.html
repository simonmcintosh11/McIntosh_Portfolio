Fixed Grid Cost Spread
PG&E bundled residential rate, illustrative model

When solar replaces utility generation, the grid bill lands on everyone else

About 42% of PG&E's average residential rate buys electricity. When a household switches to its own solar, PG&E simply buys less power, so that part of the rate stays the same per kWh. The other 58% (poles and wires, wildfire work, transmission, taxes, insurance and state programs) costs the same no matter how many customers buy power. The squeeze

Width is customers. Height is cents per kWh. The colored grid block is PG&E's total grid bill, so its area never changes. Drag its right edge left: it gets narrower, so it has to get taller. The generation band stays the same height.

Rate per kWh as customers leave

The curve bends upward because the fixed grid portion is divided by a shrinking customer base while generation stays flat. Higher rates make solar a better deal for the customers who remain, so more of them switch, which raises rates again. Analysts call this the utility "death spiral."

Data. Cost shares are from PG&E's state-required (AB 3264) breakout of the average bundled residential electric rate as of December 31, 2025: energy supply 41.8%, distribution 23.2%, wildfire-related 14.3%, transmission 5.8%, taxes 4.0%, public purpose programs incl. energy efficiency 4.5%, insurance incl. wildfire fund 2.1%, other delivery 4.2% (other, new service connections, non-wildfire emergency work, past-due recovery, metering). Starting rate is PG&E's residential bundled non-CARE average of 40.60¢/kWh effective March 1, 2026.
Simplifications. Customers who switch to solar are treated as no longer buying any PG&E power, and each remaining customer uses the same 500 kWh/month. Generation cost per kWh is held constant because PG&E buys proportionally less energy. In reality, PG&E has long-term contracts and owned plants it still pays for, which would push rates up further. The Base Services Charge (about $24/month) now recovers some fixed costs as a flat fee, which this model folds back into the per-kWh rate.
